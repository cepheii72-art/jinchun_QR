Visits and Legacy of Eminent Figures in the Late Joseon Period

Amid the political turmoil caused by foreign incursions in the final years of the Joseon dynasty, Chopyeong in Jincheon was a suitable place for intellectuals to live in seclusion. Not only was the area home to the Gyeongju Yi clan, but Wanwigak, with its extensive collection of books, also provided an ideal setting for cultivating their scholarship. Scholar-officials who had served in government in Seoul, particularly prominent figures of the Soron political faction, gathered in Chopyeong.

A substantial collection of books remained carefully preserved at Wanwigak even until the end of the Joseon dynasty. In a poem written in 1922, Jeong In-pyo wrote, “Ten thousand volumes, bound in silk, fill the bookshelves.” The verse reflects how Wanwigak continued to be regarded as a place where intellectuals could pursue scholarship and cultivate themselves.

Among the distinguished figures who resigned from government positions in Seoul and moved to Chopyeong were Hong Seung-heon (1854–?), Jeong Won-ha (1855–?), Jeong Eun-jo (1856–?), Jeong In-pyo, Yi Jong-hyeong, and Jeong In-bo. They were leading intellectuals of their time and prominent figures of the Soron faction, and shared a connection with the Ganghwa School. Hong Seung-heon, a fifth-generation descendant of Hong Yang-ho (1724–1802), resigned from his post as Vice Minister of Personnel and moved to Jincheon. Jeong Won-ha, a sixth-generation descendant of Jeong Je-du (1649–1736), regarded as a founder of Yangmingism in Joseon, resigned from his post as Inspector General and withdrew to Jincheon. Yi Jong-hyeong, the eldest male-line descendant of Yi Si-bal, left his position as Royal Secretary and returned to his ancestral home in Chopyeong. His close friend, Jeong In-pyo, moved from Anseong to Chopyeong in 1898. Jeong Eun-jo, who had also served as Royal Secretary, later moved to Chopyeong with his son, Jeong In-bo. These figures helped guide and educate Jeong In-bo, who was still in his teens at the time.

Thus, in the final years of the Joseon dynasty, Wanwigak was more than a repository for books. It served as an important cultural space for intellectuals of the Soron tradition and as an academic and intellectual center for prominent scholars and literati.
