The Life of Yi Ha-gon
Origins and Early Life (1677–1707)
Born in Yangchon, Chopyeong-myeon, Jincheon-
gun, Chungcheongbuk-do Province, 
Yi Ha-gon cultivated his scholarship and artistic 
talents from an early age. His early poems in 
Dutacho celebrated nature and the landscapes of his 
hometown.
A Turning Point (1708)
After passing the civil service examination and 
earning the title of jinsa (literary licentiate), 
Yi Ha-gon chose to withdraw from public life rather 
than pursue an official career
and devoted himself to scholarship and the arts.
	
Seclusion and Scholarship (From 1709)
In Jincheon, Yi Ha-gon built Mangwollu, a pavilion 
that housed 10,000 books. 
There, he immersed himself in reading and 
scholarship while pursuing calligraphy and painting.
Travels and Excursions (1710s)
Yi Ha-gon traveled extensively across Korea, 
including to Geumgangsan Mountain and the Honam 
region. 
During his journeys, he visited Buddhist temples 
and captured his impressions of nature in poetry and 
painting.
Death and Legacy (1724)
Yi Ha-gon died in 1724, the year King Yeongjo 
ascended the throne.
He left behind Dutacho, along with a substantial 
body of poetry, prose, calligraphy, paintings, and 
commentary. He is remembered as a quintessential 
literatus of the late Joseon dynasty who left a lasting 
mark on the history of Korean literature and art.

The Evolution of the Book
Handwritten Manuscripts
With the invention of writing, knowledge, 
observations, and records of daily life were 
preserved and passed down through hand-copied 
manuscripts.
Woodblock Printing
As civilization advanced and education flourished, 
demand grew for books that could be produced 
in large quantities without errors. This led to the 
transition from the manuscript era to the age of 
printed books.
Movable Type Printing
Book production evolved further with movable type, 
where individual characters were cast, arranged, and 
typeset to produce printed pages.
Electronic Books
With the advent of smartphones, tablets, and 
e-readers, books have taken on various digital 
forms, expanding the ways we read and enriching 
the reading experience.

An Anecdote Concerning 
Wanwigak
Yi Ha-gon’s zeal for acquiring fine calligraphy and 
paintings was extraordinary. It is said that if he 
encountered a masterpiece while traveling, he would 
even trade the clothes off his back to obtain it.
One famous story involves Mangcheonjeodo, 
a painting by Jeong Seon. Yi Ha-gon and Shin 
Jeong-ha competed to acquire the painting, and Yi 
ultimately prevailed. The episode is recorded in his 
literary collection, Dutacho:
“When I look at this album, the brushwork is utterly 
free of vulgarity, and it deserves to be treasured. 
Ilwon does not understand painting and is therefore 
unfit to keep it, though his poetry is beautiful 
enough to complement the painting. [...]
In Wanwigak Pavilion, there are only a few dozen old 
paintings, none by recent artists. This shows that 
I have not collected many paintings and have not 
been eager to acquire them. Yet Jeongbo mocks 
me, saying that although I already possess many, I 
still covet more. He hopes that Ilwon’s affection for 
the painting will diminish so that he can profit from 
the situation, like the fisherman who benefits from 
the struggle between the snipe and the clam. But 
Ilwon is no child. Why would he let such a treasure 
slip away into the clouds and mist?
Jeongbo’s old habit has returned once again. I can 
only laugh.”
Wonbaek was the courtesy name of Jeong Seon 
(1676–1759), the painter of Mangcheonjeodo. Ilwon 
was the courtesy name of Yi Byeong-yeon (1671–
1751), who owned the painting at the time. Jeongbo 
was the courtesy name of Shin Jeong-ha (1681–
1716), who vied with Yi Ha-gon to acquire the 
painting. In his attempt to secure the painting, Shin 
Jeong-ha disparaged Yi Byeong-yeon as someone 
who did not understand painting and accused Yi Ha-
gon of coveting yet another work despite already 
owning many.
Yi Ha-gon and Yi Byeong-yeon were fellow disciples 
of Kim Chang-hyeop and Kim Chang-heup. Shin 
Jeong-ha was a pupil of Bak Se-chae and was also 
close to Yi Byeong-yeon. Jeong Seon frequented 
Kim Chang-heup’s circle as a painter. The contest 
over Mangcheonjeodo was therefore less a serious 
conflict than a playful exchange among closely 
connected literati.

The Establishment of 
Wanwigak
Wanwigak was a library pavilion established by Yi 
Ha-gon (1677–1724) in Yangji Village, Chopyeong-
myeon, Jincheon-gun. Wanwigak, believed to have 
been established by 1713, when Yi Ha-gon was 
37, was regarded as one of the four great library 
pavilions of the Joseon dynasty. It was a cultural 
space where distinguished figures visited to read 
books and appreciate paintings. It housed a large 
collection of rare and valuable books and was 
popularly known as Mangwollu, or “Pavilion of Ten 
Thousand Volumes.”
The Gyeongju Yi clan first settled in Chopyeong-
myeon through Yi Ha-gon’s great-grandfather, Yi 
Si-bal (1569–1626). After helping suppress the 
Japanese invasions of Korea and Yi Gwal’s Rebellion, 
Yi Si-bal was granted land in the Chopyeong area, 
where his descendants subsequently put down 
roots.
Yi Ha-gon had an exceptional love of books and 
rarely put them down. At Wanwigak, he collected 
not only Confucian classics and historical works, but 
also fiction, medical texts, and books on divination. 
In one of his poems, he compared himself to a 
bookworm:
“This old man, too, has a fondness for books;
I know that in a former life I must have been two 
bookworms.”
The late Joseon scholar Yi Yu-won (1814–1888) 
traced the origins of Wanwigak to an anecdote 
concerning Yi Ha-gon’s ancestor, Yi Je-hyeon 
(1287–1367). While in Yanjing during the Yuan 
dynasty, Yi Je-hyeon established a reading hall 
called Mangwondang, or “Hall of Ten Thousand 
Volumes,” and stocked it with numerous books from 
ancient and contemporary times. There, he pursued 
scholarly studies and engaged in discussions with 
renowned Yuan scholars of the time, including Zhao 
Mengfu and Yuan Mingshan.
Instead of simply owning books, Yi Ha-gon carefully 
proofread and critiqued each volume, and whenever 
he encountered a book he wanted, particularly one 
not already in his collection, he spared no means 
or effort to acquire it. This extraordinary devotion 
ultimately gave rise to the rich and diverse collection 
of books at Wanwigak.

==================== PAGE 6 ====================
Records of Wanwigak by 
Distinguished Figures of the 
Joseon Dynasty
As Wanwigak became known for its extensive 
collection of rare and valuable books, its reputation 
spread widely, attracting distinguished figures both 
during Yi Ha-gon’s lifetime and in later generations.
The renowned calligrapher Yun Sun (1680–1741) 
wrote the plaque inscription “Wanwigak,” while his 
disciple Yi Gwang-sa (1705–1777) wrote the plaque 
inscription “Mangwollu.” During Yi Ha-gon’s lifetime, 
Kim Chang-heup and Yi Byeong-yeon, who were 
closely associated with him, mentioned Wanwigak in 
their writings. In later generations, figures including 
Sim Yuk (1685–1753), Yi Chung-ik (1744–1816), and 
Yi Yu-won (1814–1888) left poems and prose about 
the pavilion.
When Yi Jip-du, Yi Ha-gon’s grandson, was 
appointed magistrate of Jincheon County and 
brought his father, Yi Seok-no, to live with him, Yi 
Chung-ik composed a poem for the occasion. In it, 
he noted that Wanwigak contained a remarkably rich 
collection of books and that Yi Jip-du planned to 
remain there after completing his term in office to 
preserve the scholarly legacy of his ancestors.
Yi Yu-won (1814–1888) wrote in his essay 
Mangwollu:
“In Chopyeong, there is Mangwollu, which was 
established by Yi Ha-gon.”
He explained that it was given this name because it 
housed a large collection of books covering a wide 
range of subjects.

Visits and Legacy of Eminent 
Figures in the Late Joseon 
Period
Amid the political turmoil caused by foreign 
incursions in the final years of the Joseon dynasty, 
Chopyeong in Jincheon was a suitable place for 
intellectuals to live in seclusion. Not only was the 
area home to the Gyeongju Yi clan, but Wanwigak, 
with its extensive collection of books, also provided 
an ideal setting for cultivating their scholarship. 
Scholar-officials who had served in government in 
Seoul, particularly prominent figures of the Soron 
political faction, gathered in Chopyeong. 
A substantial collection of books remained carefully 
preserved at Wanwigak even until the end of the 
Joseon dynasty. In a poem written in 1922, Jeong 
In-pyo wrote, “Ten thousand volumes, bound in 
silk, fill the bookshelves.” The verse reflects how 
Wanwigak continued to be regarded as a place 
where intellectuals could pursue scholarship and 
cultivate themselves.
Among the distinguished figures who resigned 
from government positions in Seoul and moved 
to Chopyeong were Hong Seung-heon (1854–?), 
Jeong Won-ha (1855–?), Jeong Eun-jo (1856–?), 
Jeong In-pyo, Yi Jong-hyeong, and Jeong In-bo. 
They were leading intellectuals of their time and 
prominent figures of the Soron faction, and shared a 
connection with the Ganghwa School. Hong Seung-
heon, a fifth-generation descendant of Hong Yang-
ho (1724–1802), resigned from his post as Vice 
Minister of Personnel and moved to Jincheon. 
Jeong Won-ha, a sixth-generation descendant of 
Jeong Je-du (1649–1736), regarded as a founder 
of Yangmingism in Joseon, resigned from his post 
as Inspector General and withdrew to Jincheon. Yi 
Jong-hyeong, the eldest male-line descendant of 
Yi Si-bal, left his position as Royal Secretary and 
returned to his ancestral home in Chopyeong. His 
close friend, Jeong In-pyo, moved from Anseong 
to Chopyeong in 1898. Jeong Eun-jo, who had 
also served as Royal Secretary, later moved to 
Chopyeong with his son, Jeong In-bo. These figures 
helped guide and educate Jeong In-bo, who was 
still in his teens at the time. Thus, in the final years 
of the Joseon dynasty, Wanwigak was more than 
a repository for books. It served as an important 
cultural space for intellectuals of the Soron tradition 
and as an academic and intellectual center for 
prominent scholars and literati.

Genealogy of the Gyeongju Yi 
Clan of Chopyeong, Jincheon
[Reference 1]
The Gyeongju Yi clan of Chopyeong traces its 
lineage to the eight sons of Yi Gong-rin, collectively 
known as the Palbyeol (八鼈, Eight Turtles). Yi Gong-
rin, a sixth-generation descendant of Yi Je-hyeon, 
married a daughter of Bak Paeng-nyeon. According 
to legend, he dreamed of eight turtles, said to be 
the sons of the Dragon King. When his eight sons 
were born, he named them after aquatic creatures: 
O (鰲, Giant Turtle), Gu (龜, Turtle), Won (黿, Softshell 
Turtle), Ta (鼉, Crocodile), Byeol (鼈, Softshell Turtle), 
Byeok (鼊, Turtle), Gyeong (鯨, Whale), and Gon (鯤, 
Giant Fish). 
[Reference 2]
The third of the eight brothers, Yi Won, was an 
ancestor of Yi Ha-gon. Yi Won’s third son, Yi 
Gyeong-yun, had three sons. Through Yi Dae-geon, 
one of his descendants, the family line reached 
Yi Si-bal, who settled in Chopyeong, Jincheon. 
The lineage continued through Yi Si-bal’s son, 
Yi Gyeong-eok, and his grandson, Yi In-yeop, 
eventually leading to Yi Ha-gon. After Yi Ha-gon, 
the family line continued through Yi Seok-pyo, Yi 
Hu-won, and Yi Jip-ro.

Donor Yi Jeong-hui
Born in Yongjeong-ri, Chopyeong-myeon, Jincheon-
gun, Chungcheongbuk-do Province, Yi Jeong-hui 
was a tenth-generation descendant of Yi In-yeop of 
the Gyeongju Yi clan. To support the restoration of 
Wanwigak and the development of Chopyeong Book 
Village, he donated some 800 old books that had 
been preserved by his family. Some of the books in 
the original collection were destroyed in a fire in the 
late Joseon period. The surviving volumes continued 
to be preserved in the family’s sarangchae, the 
traditional men’s quarters of the house, and were 
kept even after the family moved to Seoul. At one 
point, the collection was also entrusted to the 
Jangseogak Archives of the Academy of Korean 
Studies. The donated books will be permanently 
preserved by Jincheon County and continue to serve 
as valuable resources for exhibitions and academic 
research.
